I'm designing a physics replication study and want your critique of the design before I lock it. Context: avian magnetoreception via radical pairs in cryptochrome-4 (Cry4). I have a validated Haberkorn master-equation solver (closed-form singlet yield, recombination rates from a structure-derived back-electron-transfer lifetime model, W395-W372-W318-W369 chain from PDB 6PTZ) that reproduces the Ritz theta curve and the Hiscock 2016 field-strength response. Xu et al. 2021 (Nature 594, "Magnetic sensitivity of cryptochrome 4 from a migratory songbird") measured in vitro that robin erCry4 is more magnetically sensitive than chicken GgCry4 and pigeon ClCry4, that the 4th Trp (W369/TrpD) prolongs the magnetically sensitive state, and that W369F (WDF) triad mutants show larger but transient magnetic field effects.

Proposed study: simulate full MFE-vs-field-strength curves (0-200 uT) for erCry4, ClCry4, GgCry4 wild-type with my solver, plus the erCry4 WDF virtual mutant, using each species' actual chain-edge distances from predicted structures. Pre-registered gates: (a) predicted WT sensitivity ordering matches Xu's measured ordering (erCry4 above both non-migratory, Cl/Gg comparable); (b) virtual WDF mutant reproduces the direction of Xu's measured mutant effect (larger short-time, smaller long-time response); (c) the discriminating physical quantity is identified a priori (lifetime-weighted sensitivity integral, not peak amplitude).

Questions: 1) Is this a defensible positive replication design, or does it just recover what I put in (circularity risk - e.g. my lifetime anchor already encodes the tetrad advantage)? 2) What would make the gates convincing to a science-fair judge vs. trivially satisfiable? 3) What is the strongest single weakness to fix before locking? Be direct and critical.

Yes—but in its current form I would not lock it as a “positive replication.” I would call it a model-based replication with a substantial risk of circularity. The good news is that the core idea can be made much stronger without abandoning your solver.

Xu et al. did report that ErCRY4 showed greater magnetic sensitivity than chicken and pigeon CRY4, and their experiments explicitly examined the successive flavin–tryptophan radical pairs and the role of the fourth tryptophan. 
Nature

1. Where the circularity risk actually is

Your biggest issue isn't that you're testing a prediction that happens to agree with Xu. That's exactly what a replication should do.

The problem is where the information determining your prediction enters the model.

You have roughly:

structure → Trp-chain geometry → back-electron-transfer lifetime → Haberkorn dynamics → MFE curve → sensitivity ranking

If your structure-derived lifetime model was calibrated or selected using information that already distinguishes the fourth-Trp-containing ErCRY4 system, then the final Er > Cl/Gg result isn't fully independent.

The especially dangerous case is:

“W369 extends the lifetime, and longer lifetime produces greater sensitivity, therefore ErCRY4 should be more sensitive.”

That would make the replication partly tautological.

Xu's paper itself makes the fourth-Trp issue mechanistically important, so you aren't inventing an irrelevant variable after seeing the result. 
Nature
 But that also means you need to establish that your lifetime model predicts the relevant lifetime from structural information independently of the Xu phenotype.

There's another subtle issue: field sensitivity is not simply monotonic with lifetime. Radical-pair sensitivity depends on the competition between spin evolution, recombination, relaxation/decoherence, hyperfine interactions and the external field. Published theoretical work emphasizes that the characteristic field scale and sensitivity depend strongly on these competing processes. 
PubMed
+1

So your project becomes much more interesting if you demonstrate:

the structure-derived kinetic differences quantitatively generate the experimentally observed species differences without fitting those differences to the Xu data.

That's a genuine mechanistic replication.

2. Your current gates aren't strong enough
Gate A

WT ordering matches Xu.

Good as a primary replication endpoint, but weak as a standalone gate.

Why? Because there are only three species, and you're essentially asking whether:

Er > Cl/Gg.

A model with enough adjustable kinetic freedom could potentially produce that ordering without being especially predictive.

I'd strengthen it substantially.

Instead of only asking whether the ordering matches, preregister:

A1 — ordering

ErCRY4 > ClCRY4 and ErCRY4 > GgCRY4.

A2 — effect-size prediction

Predict the quantitative separation between species using the model without fitting to Xu's measured amplitudes.

Gate B is currently problematic

Your WDF mutant test is potentially your strongest experiment, but your formulation gives yourself too much freedom:

“larger short-time, smaller long-time response.”

That's qualitatively easy to satisfy.

You should preregister the crossover behavior.

For example:

Then the model has to get a time-dependent phenotype, rather than merely producing “bigger” somewhere.

That's considerably more compelling.

Xu et al. specifically investigated the roles of successive Trp radical pairs and found that the fourth Trp affects the persistence/stabilization of the magnetically sensitive state, making this a mechanistically meaningful test rather than just another parameter perturbation.

3. I would change Gate C the most

You wrote:

lifetime-weighted sensitivity integral, not peak amplitude.

That's scientifically sensible—but it is dangerous to declare it the discriminating quantity if you chose it after understanding how your model behaves.

A judge could reasonably ask:

“Why this integral? Did you choose it because it happens to make the replication work?”

You need to turn C into a predefined mechanistic observable, not an optimization target.

For example, preregister:

“The primary scalar phenotype is the integral of the absolute magnetic-field response over the experimentally relevant observation interval and field range, because the hypothesized signaling state depends on both magnitude and persistence of the MFE.”

Then freeze the integration bounds and normalization before running the species comparison.

Even better: don't make the integral your only metric.

Have:

Primary: pre-registered lifetime-weighted sensitivity integral.

Secondary: peak MFE.

Secondary: characteristic field scale.

Secondary: persistence/decay time.

Exploratory: alternative integrals/normalizations.

If only your specially selected integral reproduces Xu while the other metrics don't, that itself is important evidence about model dependence.

The strongest improvement: blind the model to the phenotype

This is what I'd do if this were my science-fair project.

Lock the model first.

Before examining the Xu quantitative data:

Freeze:

Hamiltonian formulation

hyperfine parameters

g tensors

recombination model

structure-selection procedure

distance extraction method

lifetime equation

all rate constants

initial conditions

field grid

angular averaging

integration windows

sensitivity metric

W369F structural treatment

numerical tolerances

Then generate the predictions.

Only afterward reveal/compare against Xu's experimental curves.

That transforms the project from:

“I built a model that reproduces Xu.”

into:

“I froze a mechanistic model independently, generated predictions for previously measured phenotypes, and then performed a blinded replication comparison.”

That's a substantially stronger claim.

One thing I would add: a negative/control prediction

This could make the project much more impressive.

Don't only ask:

Does the model recover the known result?

Ask:

Can it distinguish the known biological signal from plausible counterfactual structures?

For example, construct controlled perturbations where you alter one structural determinant at a time while holding the others fixed.

Conceptually:

Real structure

→ predicted MFE

structure with W369 contribution removed

→ predicted MFE

structure with chain-edge distance perturbed

→ predicted MFE

lifetime held artificially constant across species

→ predicted MFE

That last one is particularly powerful.

If you force identical lifetimes across Er/Cl/Gg and the species sensitivity difference largely disappears, you have evidence that:

the lifetime difference is causally responsible for the predicted interspecies effect.

If the difference remains, you've discovered that some other parameter—hyperfine coupling, geometry, anisotropy, etc.—is responsible.

That is much more scientifically interesting than simply getting the correct ordering.

The strongest single weakness to fix
Your lifetime model is currently both the engine of the prediction and potentially the source of the biological effect you're trying to replicate.

That's the Achilles' heel.

A judge could ask:

“How do you know you didn't just encode the Xu result into the lifetime?”

You need to answer that quantitatively and reproducibly.

I would therefore make the centerpiece of the methodology an ablation/independence analysis:

Model condition	Species-specific lifetime?	Species-specific structure?	Purpose
Full model	✓	✓	Main prediction
Equalized lifetimes	✗	✓	Test lifetime causality
Equalized structures/geometry	✓	✗	Test structural contribution
Equalized both	✗	✗	Baseline
W369 removed	modified	✓	Test tetrad mechanism

You don't need to invent new physics. You're asking the existing model a much more rigorous causal question.

What would make this science-fair convincing?

I'd frame the study around this hypothesis:

Can an independently parameterized radical-pair model, using only protein structure and physically motivated kinetic parameters, predict the interspecies and fourth-tryptophan dependence of cryptochrome-4 magnetic sensitivity without fitting to the experimental phenotype?

That is a much stronger research question than:

“Can my solver reproduce Xu et al.?”

Then your success criteria become:

Prediction 1: entire Er/Cl/Gg MFE-field curves.

Prediction 2: quantitative interspecies effect sizes.

Prediction 3: W369F time-dependent behavior.

Prediction 4: disappearance/reduction of the species difference when lifetime differences are experimentally/model-wise equalized.

Prediction 5: robustness to reasonable uncertainty in structural distances and kinetic parameters.

That last one is important. If tiny changes in predicted distances flip ErCRY4 from first to third, the apparent replication isn't robust.

One more important physics issue

Your 0–200 μT window needs justification.

Earth-strength fields are indeed the relevant regime for magnetoreception, but the intrinsic field scale of the radical-pair system depends on its hyperfine and kinetic parameters; theoretical work has found substantially larger characteristic field scales for some FAD–Trp radical pairs under particular assumptions. 
PubMed

So don't simply choose 0–200 μT because it contains the geomagnetic field.

Preregister why that range is appropriate for the particular observable and parameterization you're using, and ideally calculate a wider field curve as a secondary analysis. Otherwise you risk selecting a window that preferentially displays the behavior you expect.

My verdict

Core idea: strong.

Current replication design: promising but not yet sufficiently independent.

Most important change: separate the parameter-generation stage from the phenotype-comparison stage, and prove through lifetime/geometry ablations that your result isn't mathematically baked into the lifetime anchor.

If you do that, the project stops being “I reproduced a published graph with a solver” and becomes a considerably more interesting computational physics study:

Can microscopic structural/kinetic differences in a cryptochrome radical-pair system quantitatively predict experimentally observed magnetic sensitivity—and can the model identify which physical parameter actually causes the difference?

That's the version I'd lock.
